Fever

Fever is the body's normal response to infection, and many pediatric sources now say to "treat the child, not the number." A comfortable, drinking, alert child with a fever often needs only rest and fluids.

Traditional remedies

Fluids, rest, and light clothing

Good evidence
How to make & use
Offer small sips often (water, diluted juice, broth, ice pops, breast milk or formula for babies). Dress the child in one light layer and use a light sheet. Keep the room comfortably cool.
Tradition
Standard home care everywhere.
What the research says
Pediatric guidance (Cleveland Clinic, AAP) puts comfort and hydration first. Bundling up to "sweat out" a fever can push a small child's temperature higher. [61] Cleveland Clinic; [62] AAP HealthyChildren

Lukewarm (not cold) washcloth or bath

Some evidence
How to make & use
Place a cool, damp washcloth on the forehead or neck for comfort. A lukewarm bath (not cold, no alcohol) is fine if the child likes it. Stop if they shiver.
Tradition
Sponging the body is an old nursing practice. Many families used vinegar or alcohol rubs.
What the research says
UK NICE guidance says tepid sponging is not recommended for bringing fever down. A cool cloth for comfort is fine. Cold baths and alcohol rubs are unsafe. [63] NICE (UK) guideline NG143; [61] Cleveland Clinic

Onion or garlic on the feet (socks)

Traditional use only
How to make & use
Slices of onion on the soles under socks overnight, a traditional "draw out the fever" method.
Tradition
European (German, Polish, Ukrainian) and British folk tradition.
What the research says
There are no studies supporting this, and fact-checkers rate it unsupported. Onion is harmless. Raw garlic on skin has caused chemical burns on children's feet, so don't use garlic. [50] AAP FactCheck (Australian Associated Press); [64] Pediatr Emerg Care 2019

Vinegar rubs or wraps

Traditional use only
How to make & use
A traditional rub of diluted vinegar on the body, or vinegar-soaked socks.
Tradition
Eastern European, Latin American, and Southern grandmother remedies.
What the research says
There are no studies. Any cooling comes from evaporation, like a damp cloth. It is safe only if heavily diluted, and never on broken skin. [61] Cleveland Clinic

Safety & kids

  • Never give aspirin to children or teens with a fever or viral illness, because it can cause Reye syndrome. Some older home remedies, including one community post below, recommend it.
  • No alcohol rubs, ice baths, or cold baths. Don't pile on blankets.
  • No raw garlic on skin, which has caused chemical burns.
  • For a baby under 3 months, call the doctor before trying any home treatment.

See a doctor when

  • Any temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher in a baby under 3 months: call right away or go to the ER.
  • Fever of 104°F (40°C) or higher at any age, or fever lasting more than 2–3 days in a child (5 days per NICE), or more than 3 days in adults.
  • A child who is very sleepy, hard to wake, confused, or inconsolable, or who has a seizure, a stiff neck, a purple-red rash that doesn't fade when pressed, trouble breathing, or signs of dehydration (no urine for 8 hours, no tears, dry mouth).
  • Fever that goes away and then returns, or fever after recent travel.

Safety and red-flag sources: [18] CDC; [63] NICE (UK) guideline NG143
