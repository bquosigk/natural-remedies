Skin Rashes & Itch

Bug bites, poison ivy (common around Cartersville), heat rash, eczema flares, and dry winter skin are the usual suspects.

Traditional remedies

Colloidal oatmeal bath

Some evidence
How to make & use
Grind 1 cup plain oats very fine in a blender (or use a store packet) and stir into a lukewarm bath. Soak 10–15 minutes, pat dry, and moisturize.
Tradition
Oatmeal baths for skin go back to ancient Rome and Egypt.
What the research says
Colloidal oatmeal has anti-itch and anti-inflammatory compounds and is FDA-recognized as a skin protectant. [102] Reynertson 2015, J Drugs Dermatol; [103] American Academy of Dermatology

Cool compress and calamine

Some evidence
How to make & use
Apply a cool, wet cloth for 5–10 minutes. Calamine lotion works for poison ivy and bug bites.
Tradition
Calamine has been a household staple since the 1800s.
What the research says
The American Academy of Dermatology recommends cool compresses and calamine for itch. [103] American Academy of Dermatology

Baking soda bath or paste

Traditional use only
How to make & use
Add ½–1 cup baking soda to a lukewarm bath. Some make a paste for bites.
Tradition
American folk remedy.
What the research says
Recommended by the AAD in a bath for itch. As a paste it can irritate or burn some skin, so patch-test first. [103] American Academy of Dermatology

Aloe gel (heat rash, sunburn)

Traditional use only
How to make & use
Thin layers of fresh aloe gel. Keep skin cool and loose-clothed. Avoid heavy oils and lotions on heat rash.
Tradition
Traditional across warm climates.
What the research says
Soothing. Some people are allergic. [101] NIH NCCIH

Safety & kids

  • Patch-test new remedies on a small area first. Chamomile and lavender can trigger allergic skin reactions, and lavender oil has been linked to breast swelling in boys.
  • Avoid essential oils on babies' skin and on broken skin.
  • Very hot water may briefly calm itchy bites in adults but can scald children, so don't use it for kids, and never use it on hives.

See a doctor when

  • Hives with swelling of the lips, tongue, or face, wheezing, or trouble breathing (call 911; possible anaphylaxis).
  • A purple-red rash that doesn't fade when pressed under a glass, especially with fever (go now).
  • Rash with fever, blisters, or skin peeling, or a rash that spreads quickly, oozes, or looks infected.
  • Poison ivy on the face, eyes, or genitals, or covering a large area. Rash with a bullseye after a tick bite.
  • Itch lasting more than 2 weeks despite home care.

Safety and red-flag sources: [103] American Academy of Dermatology
