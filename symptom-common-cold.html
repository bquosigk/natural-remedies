Common Cold (general care and prevention)

Colds are viral and run their course in 7–10 days, so antibiotics don't help. Families who prefer natural care mostly focus on rest, fluids, warm food, and remedies to shorten or prevent the cold.

Traditional remedies

Rest, fluids, and chicken soup

Some evidence
How to make & use
Offer warm fluids often, plus homemade chicken soup with vegetables, garlic, and onion.
Tradition
Chicken soup for colds goes back at least to the 12th-century physician Maimonides and is a staple of Jewish, Southern, and many other kitchens.
What the research says
A lab study found chicken soup had mild anti-inflammatory effects, and hot fluids ease symptoms. It is low-risk and comforting. [22] Rennard 2000, Chest; [6] Sanu & Eccles 2008, Rhinology

Raw garlic and garlic honey

Traditional use only
How to make & use
Crush 1–2 cloves and let them sit 10 minutes. Eat on buttered bread or toast, or stir into soup at the end of cooking. Fermented garlic honey: cover peeled cloves with raw honey in a jar, burp it daily, and use after 3–4 weeks, about 1 tsp a day (age 1+).
Tradition
Used for colds in European, Mediterranean, Russian, and Chinese folk medicine.
What the research says
A Cochrane review found only one small trial, which suggested fewer colds. That is insufficient evidence. NCCIH says garlic hasn't been shown to help colds. [42] Cochrane review; [43] NIH NCCIH

Elderberry syrup

Some evidence
How to make & use
Simmer ⅔ cup dried elderberries in 3½ cups water with 2 Tbsp grated ginger, 1 tsp cinnamon, and ½ tsp cloves for about 30–45 minutes until reduced by half. Mash, strain, cool to lukewarm, then stir in 1 cup raw honey. Refrigerate and use within about 2 weeks. A common blog dose is ½–1 tsp for kids and ½–1 Tbsp for adults, daily during cold season or several times a day when sick.
Tradition
European folk medicine ("the medicine chest of the country people"). Native American nations used American elder, which grows wild in Georgia.
What the research says
A small meta-analysis found elderberry reduced upper-respiratory symptoms, and one trial found shorter colds in air travelers. NCCIH calls the evidence insufficient because studies are small and many were industry-funded. [44] Hawkins 2019, Complement Ther Med; [45] Tiralongo 2016, Nutrients; [46] NIH NCCIH; [47] Wellness Mama blog

Zinc (lozenges)

Some evidence
How to make & use
Adults: zinc acetate or gluconate lozenges, started within 24 hours of the first symptoms. Follow the label and do not exceed it.
Tradition
A modern remedy.
What the research says
NCCIH says zinc may shorten colds when started early. Side effects include nausea and a bad taste. Never use zinc nasal sprays, which have caused permanent loss of smell. [4] NIH NCCIH; [48] NIH Office of Dietary Supplements

Vitamin C

Some evidence
How to make & use
Food sources (citrus, peppers, kiwi), or a modest supplement.
Tradition
Popularized by Linus Pauling in the 1970s.
What the research says
A Cochrane review found no benefit from starting vitamin C after a cold begins. Taking it regularly slightly shortens colds and helps people under heavy physical stress. [49] Cochrane review

Onion by the bed or in socks

Traditional use only
How to make & use
Slice an onion. Put a half on a plate near the bed, or put slices on the soles of the feet under socks overnight.
Tradition
Often traced to medieval European plague-era beliefs that onions "absorb" illness. Popular in Germany, Eastern Europe, and the UK.
What the research says
There are no studies, and there is no known mechanism by which onions absorb germs or toxins. Fact-checkers rate the claim unsupported. It is harmless if you don't mind the smell. [50] AAP FactCheck (Australian Associated Press)

Jen's Elderberry Syrup (local vendor)

Some evidence
What they share
The store's "Local" page lists Jen's Elderberry Syrup among the local vendors it carries. I found no store instructions on how to use it.

Follow the bottle label. Check whether it is sweetened with honey.

What the research says
Same as elderberry above: small positive trials, but NCCIH calls the evidence insufficient. [44] Hawkins 2019, Complement Ther Med; [46] NIH NCCIH

  • If it contains honey, it is not for babies under 12 months.
  • Commercial syrups are cooked, which removes the toxic compounds in raw berries. Pregnancy safety is unknown.

Turmeric Elixir and ginger juices (café)

Traditional use only
What they share
The store's juice-program menu describes ginger and turmeric (Turmeric Elixir: turmeric, ginger, lemon, coconut water; Sweet and Spicy juice: ginger, carrot, orange, cucumber) as "power foods… great to take during cold and flu season."
How they suggest using it
Fresh-pressed drinks from the café. No dose given.
Store source
[51] Nature's Corner Market – Juice Detox Program Menu (PDF, updated Nov 2025); [52] Nature's Corner Market – Café/Juice Bar page (wellness elixirs, wheatgrass shots)
What the research says
NCCIH: there isn't enough evidence to say turmeric or curcumin helps any condition other than a few non-cold uses. Ginger and citrus are comforting and hydrating. [53] NIH NCCIH; [5] NIH NCCIH

Safety & kids

  • Food amounts in a drink are fine for most people. Turmeric supplements (not the juice) have been linked to rare liver injury, may interact with blood thinners, and should be avoided with gallbladder problems.

"Basic" cold-season vitamin protocol from a guest talk hosted by the store (vitamins A, D, zinc, C; plus echinacea and elderberry)

Some evidence
What they share
In a 2021 talk posted on the store's YouTube channel, guest speaker Dr. Rhet Bergeron listed "a, d, zinc, and c" as "the basic protocol for any person who feels like they're healthy," mainly in "the colder months." He gave adult amounts of 10,000–20,000 IU each for vitamins A and D, and said that for a 10-year-old "we could probably cut these in half." His "expanded" list for people who are immune-compromised included echinacea, elderberry, probiotics, greens, and omega-3s.
How they suggest using it
These are the adult amounts as spoken in the talk. Do not use them for children. See the cautions below.
Store source
[54] Nature's Corner Market YouTube
What the research says
Zinc lozenges may shorten colds if started early. Regular vitamin C slightly shortens colds. Echinacea shows at most a small preventive effect (Cochrane). Vitamin A has no evidence for colds in well-nourished people. Vitamin D at normal doses is reasonable if you are low, but the high doses here are not evidence-based for colds. [4] NIH NCCIH; [49] Cochrane review; [55] Cochrane review; [56] NIH NCCIH; [57] NIH Office of Dietary Supplements; [58] NIH Office of Dietary Supplements

Safety & kids

  • The doses in this talk exceed NIH safe upper limits. Adult upper limits are 3,000 mcg (10,000 IU) of preformed vitamin A and 4,000 IU of vitamin D a day. For children, the vitamin A limits are 600 mcg (about 2,000 IU) at ages 1–3, 900 mcg (about 3,000 IU) at ages 4–8, and 1,700 mcg (about 5,700 IU) at ages 9–13. The vitamin D limits are 2,500 IU at ages 1–3, 3,000 IU at ages 4–8, and 4,000 IU from age 9. Halving the adult doses for a 10-year-old still goes over both limits.
  • High-dose vitamin A can cause birth defects, so avoid it in pregnancy, and it can damage the liver. Too much vitamin D raises blood calcium.
  • Long-term zinc above 40 mg a day for adults (lower for kids) can cause copper deficiency.
  • Echinacea can trigger allergies, especially in people allergic to ragweed.
  • Have levels tested and dosed by your own clinician if you want to go beyond a standard multivitamin.
  • The same talk also covered COVID, vaccines, and prescription drugs (ivermectin, hydroxychloroquine). That material is not included in this guide.

Safety & kids

  • No honey under 12 months, including in elderberry syrup and garlic honey. For babies, make elderberry syrup with maple syrup or skip it.
  • Elderberries must be cooked. Raw or unripe berries, leaves, stems, and bark contain cyanide-producing compounds and caused a mass poisoning in 1983 (CDC). If foraging in Georgia, be certain of identification, use only ripe dark berries, and remove all stems.
  • Garlic in amounts above food levels can increase bleeding risk with blood thinners (warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel) and may interact with some HIV drugs. NCCIH says garlic supplements may not be safe in pregnancy above food amounts.
  • Homemade garlic-in-oil must be refrigerated and thrown out after 4 days (botulism risk).
  • Little is known about elderberry safety in pregnancy, breastfeeding, or autoimmune conditions.

See a doctor when

  • Symptoms lasting more than 10 days, or improving and then worsening.
  • Trouble breathing, ear pain, a fever above 104°F, or a fever lasting more than 3 days in a child.
  • Any fever in a baby under 3 months.

Safety and red-flag sources: [59] CDC; [60] CDC MMWR 1984
