Upset Stomach & Nausea

Nausea from a stomach bug, motion, or pregnancy is common. The priority is keeping fluids down in small sips.

Traditional remedies

Ginger (tea, chews, real ginger ale)

Good evidence
How to make & use
Steep ½–1 inch sliced fresh ginger in hot water for 10 minutes and sip slowly. Freeze ginger tea into ice cubes for small sips. Real-ginger ale, ginger chews, or pickled ginger also work. Adults can take up to about 1 g of dried ginger a day in divided doses.
Tradition
Ginger for stomach upset is a cornerstone of Chinese, Indian (Ayurvedic), and Caribbean traditions.
What the research says
Good trial evidence for pregnancy nausea. A 2021 trial in children with gastroenteritis found ginger reduced vomiting. Most commercial ginger ale contains little real ginger. [71] Viljoen 2014, Nutr J; [72] Nocerino 2021, Aliment Pharmacol Ther; [5] NIH NCCIH

Peppermint tea

Traditional use only
How to make & use
Steep 1 tsp dried peppermint or a teabag for 5–10 minutes. Sip warm or cool.
Tradition
European and Middle Eastern herbal tradition.
What the research says
Some evidence that peppermint helps digestive symptoms, mainly irritable bowel in adults. It can worsen heartburn. [73] NIH NCCIH

Sniffing rubbing alcohol (adults)

Some evidence
How to make & use
Hold an alcohol prep pad about an inch below the nose and breathe in deeply 2–3 times. Repeat if needed.
Tradition
A nurse and paramedic trick spread through forums.
What the research says
An emergency-department trial in adults found isopropyl alcohol inhalation relieved nausea better than a placebo and even better than an anti-nausea pill. [74] Beadle 2016, Ann Emerg Med

Bland foods, tiny sips, flat soda

Some evidence
How to make & use
Once vomiting settles, start with sips of water or oral rehydration solution, then bananas, rice, applesauce, toast, and crackers.
Tradition
The "BRAT diet" was standard American advice for decades. Flat cola is an old home trick.
What the research says
Pediatricians now recommend getting back to a normal diet soon rather than a strict BRAT diet. Sugary soda is not a good rehydration drink for kids; oral rehydration solution is. [75] AAP HealthyChildren; [76] CDC MMWR 2003

Charcoal Detox Lemonade (café)

Traditional use only
What they share
From the store's café menu: "activated charcoal, squeeze fresh lemon and add a dash of local raw honey… This is great for people who are experiencing stomach distress and need quick relief."
How they suggest using it
Made to order at the café. No dose given.
Store source
[51] Nature's Corner Market – Juice Detox Program Menu (PDF, updated Nov 2025)
What the research says
Activated charcoal is an emergency poisoning treatment that works by binding substances in the gut. There are no good studies showing it relieves ordinary nausea or a stomach bug. [77] AACT/EAPCCT position paper 2005

Safety & kids

  • Activated charcoal can bind and block prescription medicines, birth control pills, and supplements. Take it at least 2 hours apart from them, and ask a pharmacist. (One Earth Clinic reader reported it seemed to absorb a family member's seizure medicine.) It can cause constipation and black stools.
  • Contains raw honey, so it is not for babies under 1. Don't give charcoal to young children without Poison Control's advice (1-800-222-1222).

Safety & kids

  • Ginger is generally safe. Large supplement doses may add to blood-thinner effects. In pregnancy, food amounts are fine, but check with your provider about supplements.
  • Peppermint oil capsules can cause heartburn. Never apply peppermint oil to infants' faces.
  • Keep rubbing alcohol away from children. It is a sniff-only remedy for adults.

See a doctor when

  • Signs of dehydration: no urine in 8 hours (6 in babies), no tears, dry mouth, sunken eyes, or unusual sleepiness.
  • Green or bloody vomit, severe belly pain (especially low on the right), or a swollen, hard belly.
  • Vomiting for more than 24 hours in a child (or any repeated vomiting in a baby under 3 months), or after a head injury.
  • Can't keep down even small sips of fluid.

Safety and red-flag sources: [76] CDC MMWR 2003
