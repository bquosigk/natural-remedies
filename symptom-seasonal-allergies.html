Seasonal Allergies (hay fever)

North Georgia's spring pine and oak pollen, summer grasses, and fall ragweed bring sneezing, itchy eyes, and a runny or stuffy nose. Unlike a cold, there is no fever, the mucus stays clear, and symptoms come back every season.

Traditional remedies

Saline nasal rinse or spray

Some evidence
How to make & use
Use a squeeze bottle or neti pot with ¼–½ tsp non-iodized salt plus a pinch of baking soda in 1 cup of distilled or boiled-and-cooled water, once or twice a day in pollen season, especially after time outdoors. Kids can use saline spray.
Tradition
Nasal washing (jala neti) comes from Ayurvedic practice.
What the research says
NCCIH says saline rinsing may be a useful addition to standard care for allergic rhinitis. [25] NIH NCCIH clinical digest; [23] U.S. FDA

Local honey

Traditional use only
How to make & use
About 1 tsp a day, starting weeks before the season (age 1+).
Tradition
An American folk belief that local pollen in honey "desensitizes" you.
What the research says
A trial found no benefit over placebo, and NCCIH calls the results inconsistent. [26] Rajan 2002, Ann Allergy Asthma Immunol; [27] NIH NCCIH

Stinging nettle tea

Traditional use only
How to make & use
Steep 1–2 tsp dried nettle leaf in 1 cup boiling water for 10 minutes. Drink 1–3 cups a day (adults).
Tradition
A European and Native American spring tonic.
What the research says
NCCIH: the evidence is inconsistent or too limited. [27] NIH NCCIH

Butterbur (PA-free extract only)

Some evidence
How to make & use
Adults only. Use only products labeled "PA-free."
Tradition
A German herbal remedy.
What the research says
NCCIH notes a review of six studies suggesting butterbur may help, but long-term safety is unproven. [25] NIH NCCIH clinical digest

From Nature’s Corner Market

What the Cartersville-area natural market has publicly shared or carries for this symptom. The store’s words are in quotation marks; see the store page for how this was gathered.

Local raw honey, started well before the season

Traditional use only
What they share
Jill (owner, naturopath) in the store's allergy-season video: "a lot of people do really well with honey… it doesn't necessarily work immediately; that's something that you typically do far in advance and it kind of works to help desensitize your body to your local problems… so that could be effective for some people, not all." (quoted from the auto-transcript, lightly cleaned up)
How they suggest using it
No amount given. The store carries local honey (its Local page lists Hometown Honey). Many people take about 1 tsp a day for several weeks before spring.
Store source
[28] Nature's Corner Market YouTube; [29] Nature's Corner Market – 'Local' vendors page (lists Jen's Elderberry Syrup, Hometown Honey, Bee Hemp ear candles and others)
What the research says
A 2002 trial found local honey did no better than placebo for hay fever. A small Finnish pilot found pre-season birch-pollen honey helped birch allergy. NCCIH calls the evidence inconsistent. Most hay-fever pollen (trees, grasses, ragweed) is wind-borne, and little of it ends up in honey. [26] Rajan 2002, Ann Allergy Asthma Immunol; [30] Saarinen 2011, Int Arch Allergy Immunol; [27] NIH NCCIH

Safety & kids

  • No honey for babies under 12 months.
  • People with pollen allergies can react to bee products (honey, bee pollen, propolis), per NCCIH. Start with a tiny amount.

Quercetin (alone or in herbal "mast-cell" combination formulas)

Traditional use only
What they share
Jill: "quercetin is a flavonoid… what a lot of people don't really realize is it's actually a natural anti[histamine]… some people do great with just taking [quercetin], but a lot of our formulas" add "the benefit of the other herbs that can kind of help calm those mast cells and calm that inflammation… so the combinations tend to be a little better." (The specific formulas aren't named.)
How they suggest using it
Follow the label. Common adult supplement doses are 500–1,000 mg a day.
Store source
[28] Nature's Corner Market YouTube
What the research says
Lab and animal studies show anti-allergy effects, and one 2022 Japanese trial found modest improvement in pollen-allergy symptoms. NCCIH lists quercetin among supplements where the evidence is "inconsistent or too limited." [31] Jafarinia 2020, Allergy Asthma Clin Immunol; [32] Yamada 2022, Eur Rev Med Pharmacol Sci; [27] NIH NCCIH

Safety & kids

  • Not studied in children or in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Ask your pediatrician first.
  • High doses have caused kidney problems in animal studies and in people given IV quercetin. Quercetin can interact with medicines processed by the liver (CYP enzymes) and some antibiotics (Andres 2018).
  • Multi-herb "allergy" combinations may contain butterbur, nettle, or other herbs with their own cautions. Read the full ingredient list.

Regional homeopathic allergy remedies (adult and children's versions)

Traditional use only
What they share
Jill: "we also have a couple of homeopathics that are geared towards our area of the country… focusing on those pollens… that affect our area of the country. We do have them for adults and we also have some great products for children as well."
How they suggest using it
Per the product label (product names not given in the video).
Store source
[28] Nature's Corner Market YouTube
What the research says
NCCIH: "There's little evidence to support homeopathy as an effective treatment for any specific health condition." FDA has not approved any homeopathic product for any use. [33] NIH NCCIH; [34] U.S. FDA

Safety & kids

  • Highly diluted products are generally low-risk. However, NCCIH notes some products labeled "homeopathic" contain substantial amounts of active ingredients, and FDA has issued warnings about some children's homeopathic products. Check the ingredients and ask your pediatrician.
  • Don't let a homeopathic remedy delay care for wheezing or asthma symptoms.

DAO enzyme (diamine oxidase) plus fewer high-histamine foods

Traditional use only
What they share
Jill: some people "may be deficient… in an enzyme called DAO, for short, diamine oxidase… it helps to break down the histamine in the foods." Her suggestions: "maybe reducing some of the high-histamine foods and/or trying the DAO enzyme and see if that benefits, especially if there is some digestive situations." She names avocados, strawberries, and nuts as foods some people handle poorly.
How they suggest using it
DAO capsules are taken just before meals, per the label.
Store source
[28] Nature's Corner Market YouTube
What the research says
This targets "histamine intolerance," which is not the same thing as pollen allergy. The supporting study was a small open-label pilot (28 adults, no placebo). There are no trials for seasonal allergies. [35] Schnedl 2019, Food Sci Biotechnol

Safety & kids

  • Not studied in children. Cutting foods from a child's diet should be done with a pediatrician or dietitian.
  • Some DAO products are made from pork kidney (relevant for dietary or religious restrictions).

Life Extension FLORASSIST® Immune & Nasal Defense (EpiCor® + L. rhamnosus CRL1505)

Some evidence
What they share
In a store video, the brand's regional sales manager (with co-owner Doug Rowland) called it "particularly good come allergy season and actually one of my top suggestions to support a healthy immune response come allergy season." The store said it would carry the product at all its locations.
How they suggest using it
Manufacturer label: 1 capsule daily.
Store source
[36] Nature's Corner Market YouTube; [37] Life Extension product page
What the research says
One 2009 adult trial of EpiCor (manufacturer-funded) found less nasal congestion in allergy season. NCCIH says the evidence on probiotics for allergies is inconsistent. [38] Moyad 2009, Adv Ther; [27] NIH NCCIH

Safety & kids

  • Adult product. Not studied in children or pregnancy.
  • EpiCor is made from brewer's yeast, so avoid it with yeast allergy. Probiotics are not advised for people who are seriously ill or immunocompromised.

Life Extension Healthy Lungs (boswellia + bael fruit, saffron, andrographis)

Traditional use only
What they share
Same store video: the rep said the formula helps "inhibit inflammatory markers that could be present in the lungs" and "relax the airways," and the store host added, "this could be good for allergy season that's coming up."
How they suggest using it
Manufacturer label: 1 capsule daily. The label says "keep out of reach of children" and advises checking with a doctor if pregnant or nursing.
Store source
[36] Nature's Corner Market YouTube; [39] Life Extension product page
What the research says
The boswellia–bael blend was tested in one small 8-week asthma trial (36 adults) run with the ingredient maker. Andrographis has a review suggesting it eases cough and sore throat in colds, though the studies are low quality. There is no evidence specific to seasonal allergies. [40] Yugandhar 2018, Phytother Res; [41] Hu 2017, PLoS One

Safety & kids

  • Adults only. Andrographis can cause allergic reactions and stomach upset, and it may lower blood pressure or interact with blood thinners and immunosuppressants. Saffron in supplement doses should be avoided in pregnancy.
  • Never use it in place of a prescribed asthma inhaler.

Safety & kids

  • No honey under 12 months. Pollen-allergic people can react to honey, bee pollen, and propolis.
  • Raw butterbur contains liver-toxic pyrrolizidine alkaloids, so use PA-free products only, and never give butterbur to children or use it in pregnancy.
  • Chamomile and echinacea teas can trigger reactions in people with ragweed allergy.
  • Use only distilled or boiled water in rinses.

See a doctor when

  • Wheezing, chest tightness, or shortness of breath (possible asthma).
  • Swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat, or trouble breathing (call 911).
  • Yellow-green mucus with facial pain and fever, or symptoms lasting more than 10 days (possible sinus infection).
  • Symptoms that disrupt a child's sleep or school. An allergist can test and discuss allergy shots or tablets.

Safety and red-flag sources: [27] NIH NCCIH
