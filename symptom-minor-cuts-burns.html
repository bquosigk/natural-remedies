Minor Cuts & Burns

Small kitchen burns, scrapes, and cuts can be handled at home. Cool water comes first for burns.

Traditional remedies

Cool running water first (burns)

Good evidence
How to make & use
Hold the burn under cool (not ice-cold) running water for 20 minutes. Remove rings and tight clothing. Then cover loosely with clean plastic wrap or a non-stick dressing.
Tradition
Modern first aid that replaced the old butter-and-flour remedies.
What the research says
This is the NHS-recommended first aid for burns. Ice, butter, and grease can make burns worse. [98] NHS

Honey (burns and scrapes)

Some evidence
How to make & use
After cooling and cleaning, spread a thin layer of honey (medical-grade manuka is best) and cover with a non-stick bandage. Change daily.
Tradition
Honey on wounds is recorded in ancient Egyptian, Greek, and Ayurvedic medicine.
What the research says
A Cochrane review found honey probably heals partial-thickness burns faster than some conventional dressings. Evidence for other wounds is mixed. [99] Cochrane review

Aloe vera gel

Some evidence
How to make & use
Split a fresh leaf from a home aloe plant and spread the clear gel, or use pure store-bought gel. Apply 2–3 times a day on minor burns and sunburn.
Tradition
Used in Egypt, Mexico, the Caribbean, and the American South.
What the research says
A systematic review found aloe may help first- and second-degree burns heal faster. [100] Maenthaisong 2007, Burns; [101] NIH NCCIH

Clean, cover, and keep moist (cuts)

Good evidence
How to make & use
Rinse with clean running water and mild soap around the wound. Press with a clean cloth to stop bleeding. Apply petroleum jelly or honey and a bandage.
Tradition
Standard care.
What the research says
Moist healing is standard. Hydrogen peroxide and alcohol harm healing tissue. [99] Cochrane review

Safety & kids

  • Don't use butter, oil, ice, toothpaste, or soy sauce on a burn before cooling it with running water.
  • Raw garlic on skin can cause chemical burns, so don't use it on wounds or burns.
  • Honey is for the outside of the body only for babies. Oral honey under 1 is the botulism risk. Many guides still avoid honey on infant wounds, so ask your pediatrician.
  • Aloe latex (the yellow layer under the skin of the leaf) should not be swallowed. Oral aloe may be unsafe in pregnancy.

See a doctor when

  • Burns larger than the child's palm, or burns on the face, hands, feet, genitals, or joints, or that are white, charred, or leathery (call 911 for large burns).
  • Any chemical or electrical burn, or any burn in a baby.
  • Cuts that gape, won't stop bleeding after 10 minutes of pressure, are deep, or came from an animal or human bite or a dirty or rusty object (tetanus question).
  • Signs of infection: spreading redness, red streaks, pus, increasing pain, or fever.
