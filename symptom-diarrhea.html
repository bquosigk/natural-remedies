Diarrhea

Most diarrhea is viral and settles in a few days. The real danger, especially in small children, is losing fluids.

Traditional remedies

Oral rehydration solution (store-bought or homemade)

Good evidence
How to make & use
Store-bought solution (Pedialyte or similar) is preferred for children. The WHO-style homemade version for older children and adults is 6 level tsp sugar plus ½ level tsp salt in 1 liter (about 4¼ cups) of clean water. Give small sips often, about 1–2 tsp every few minutes for little ones. Keep breastfeeding.
Tradition
Oral rehydration therapy came from the 1960s–70s in Bangladesh and India. The Lancet called it potentially the most important medical advance of the 20th century. Rice water and salted broths are its traditional cousins.
What the research says
Strong evidence; it is the standard of care for mild to moderate dehydration. [76] CDC MMWR 2003; [75] AAP HealthyChildren

Grated apple, applesauce, banana, and rice water

Some evidence
How to make & use
Peel and grate an apple and let it brown slightly before eating, or use applesauce. Ripe banana. Plain rice or the strained cooking water from rice.
Tradition
European grated-apple remedy (Germany, Switzerland), the American BRAT diet, and Asian congee and rice water.
What the research says
A trial of apple pectin with chamomile shortened diarrhea in children. A normal, gentle diet is now advised over strict bland diets. [78] Becker 2006; [75] AAP HealthyChildren

Probiotics and yogurt

Traditional use only
How to make & use
Plain yogurt or kefir with live cultures, or a probiotic supplement.
Tradition
Fermented milk for the gut is traditional in Eastern Europe, the Middle East, and South Asia.
What the research says
A 2020 Cochrane review found no clear evidence that probiotics shorten acute infectious diarrhea. Yogurt as food is fine. [79] Cochrane review; [80] NIH NCCIH

Black tea and chamomile tea

Traditional use only
How to make & use
Weak black tea or chamomile tea, cooled, sipped slowly (older kids and adults).
Tradition
Common European and Southern home remedies.
What the research says
Mainly useful as fluid. Chamomile was part of the pectin trial above. [78] Becker 2006

Spore-based (Bacillus) probiotics, including alongside antibiotics

Some evidence
What they share
Jill, in a store video: spore-based probiotics "have a protective protein shell that gives a great survivability into the intestine… They can actually be taken alongside an antibiotic and not be harmed like a traditional probiotic… I recommend them frequently to my clients along with a complete gut support protocol."
How they suggest using it
Per the label (no specific brand or dose given).
Store source
[81] Nature's Corner Market YouTube
What the research says
For antibiotic-associated diarrhea in children, a Cochrane review found probiotics moderately reduce the risk; most of that evidence is for other strains (L. rhamnosus GG, S. boulardii). For an ordinary stomach bug, probiotics showed no clear benefit (Cochrane 2020). There is little independent research on spore-based products. [82] Cochrane review; [79] Cochrane review; [80] NIH NCCIH

Safety & kids

  • Ask the pediatrician before using these with infants. Avoid probiotics in premature babies, in anyone seriously ill or immunocompromised, or with a central IV line, because rare blood infections have occurred.

Safety & kids

  • Avoid sugary juice, soda, and sports drinks as the main fluid for small children, because the sugar can worsen diarrhea.
  • Activated charcoal can block absorption of prescription medicines (seizure medicines, birth control, and others). Ask a pharmacist first.
  • Homemade rehydration solution must be measured exactly, because too much salt is dangerous. For babies and toddlers, use store-bought solution.

See a doctor when

  • Signs of dehydration (see Nausea), or a baby under 6 months with diarrhea.
  • Blood or mucus in the stool, black stool, or high fever.
  • Diarrhea lasting more than 2–3 days in a child (or more than a week in an adult), or severe belly pain.
  • Diarrhea after recent travel, after antibiotics, or in someone with a chronic illness.

Safety and red-flag sources: [75] AAP HealthyChildren
