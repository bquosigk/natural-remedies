Insomnia & Restlessness

A calm routine is the backbone. Herbal teas and baths are gentle additions for kids and adults.

Traditional remedies

Chamomile tea

Some evidence
How to make & use
Steep 1 teabag or 1–2 tsp dried flowers in 1 cup hot water for 5–10 minutes. Drink 30–60 minutes before bed. Children can have weaker, smaller cups.
Tradition
European (German "Kamillentee"), Egyptian, and Latin American (manzanilla) tradition. Peter Rabbit's mother gave him chamomile tea.
What the research says
A meta-analysis found chamomile improved sleep quality in adults but not clinical insomnia. [104] Hieu 2019, Phytother Res; [105] NIH NCCIH

Warm bath or shower 1–2 hours before bed

Some evidence
How to make & use
10 minutes in warm water about 1–2 hours before bedtime.
Tradition
Traditional bedtime ritual.
What the research says
A meta-analysis found that a warm bath 1–2 hours before bed helped people fall asleep faster. [106] Haghayegh 2019, Sleep Med Rev

Lavender (scent)

Traditional use only
How to make & use
A sachet of dried lavender near (not in) the bed, or a few drops of oil in a diffuser in another room. Adults only for direct inhalation.
Tradition
English and Provençal tradition.
What the research says
Small studies suggest some calming effect from inhalation. The evidence is limited. [107] NIH NCCIH; [108] NIH NCCIH

Routine, dim lights, and screens off

Good evidence
How to make & use
The same bedtime every night, a bath, books, and a dark, cool room, with screens off 1 hour before bed.
Tradition
Modern sleep-hygiene advice.
What the research says
This is first-line advice for children's sleep problems. [109] NIH NCCIH

Safety & kids

  • Melatonin is a hormone supplement, not a herb, and gummies often contain more or less than the label says. Ask a pediatrician before using it in children. Store it out of reach, because child overdoses are rising.
  • Chamomile can cause allergic reactions (especially in people allergic to ragweed) and may interact with warfarin.
  • Don't put essential oils in a baby's room or on their skin.

See a doctor when

  • Loud snoring, pauses in breathing, or gasping at night (possible sleep apnea).
  • Sleep problems lasting more than a few weeks, affecting school or mood, or with anxiety or low mood.
  • Night-time restlessness with leg discomfort, or itching that keeps a child awake.

Safety and red-flag sources: [109] NIH NCCIH
